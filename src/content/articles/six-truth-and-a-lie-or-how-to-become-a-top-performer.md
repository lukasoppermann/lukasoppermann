While I am not old yet, I am also not young anymore. I worked as a freelancer, a contractor, a consultant, a small business owner, and a full-time employee for years and in many different companies. Through this I gained experience in different work environments and company cultures.

Over the years I learned things from friends, colleagues, and the internet. Things that are supposed to make you a top performer*. I tested a lot of them in the field. Not all were great, but some are gold and I want to share them with you.

I’m by no means saying those are the only things to pay attention to. Neither can I say that this will work for every person in every company. But I’ve had great success with those truths.

I have learned that most things that really change your life are not complex. They are not mysterious concepts that take years to understand. Quite the opposite. I found the things that really impact my life are simple, obvious, and straight forward ideas. The hard part is acting on them. And continuing to do so until they become second nature.

If you like one of the points, try it out, even if you think this only works in other companies. There is no way to find this out without trying. Just because nobody is doing something does not mean it won’t work. If you are unsure, start with a low stakes project to test them on.

These truths stay true. No matter if you just started your career or if you are a veteran in your field.

**A top performer is someone who drives results and takes responsibility for them*[*¹*](https://www.staffingadvisors.com/blog/what-exactly-is-a-top-performer/#:~:text=A%20top%20performer%20is%20someone,norms%20of%20your%20company%20culture)

## 1. Have a bias for action and impact

One of the most important things you can change is to focus on actions and impact. Especially in larger companies there is often too much talk and little to no action. People are afraid of failing and thus try to stay safe in stagnation. Either on purpose or by trying to plan and discuss every last detail before they start, making sure to root out all uncertainty.

As you may have guessed, this is impossible. The result will be endless meetings to plan and align. With the outcomes being more meetings to clarify points that came up.

> *In a world of stagnation, you can move mountains by having a bias for action.*

I am not suggesting you jump into the doing part immediately. Having *some* meetings and doing *some* planning makes for a smooth-running project. But after the initial meeting you should start with actions. If you start working on the project early, you have time to make mistakes and adjust. This is important because many problems come up while you work on a project. Those problems are unknown, so you can’t prevent them with better planning.

It’s also easier to estimate the required work once you have started tackling a problem. So, I would encourage you to also favor action over endless planning. While it may not work in all cases, it does work in most. Even with complex things like deciding on a technology platform, you can focus on action. For example, by trying to work with the favored platform for a few weeks. This allows you to gain valuable insights and make a better decision than if you would have spent the time in meetings and discussions.

However, often there is another problem: you have too much work. You are focusing on actions but it’s hard to make a dent because you are overwhelmed with endless tasks. In this case you must allocate your time wisely to make sure you spend it on the tasks that bring the most value. Another way to phrase it is to **focus on impact**. The goal is to focus on the tasks that are important and not on the ones that are urgent.

Since this is a common problem there are many methods to deal with this. I like to combine two methods to figure out which of the tasks has the most **impact**.

## Prioritize tasks

First you need to find out which of your tasks has the most impact. Sometimes you just know it. But if you are not sure, I love to use the value vs. effort matrix[²](https://theproductmanager.com/topics/value-vs-effort-matrix/). I find this makes it relatively easy to gauge the impact of a task. The two required measures, time and value, should be something you know about your own tasks.

The **big projects** and the **quick wins** are the impactful tasks. Knowing those you can use the Eisenhower Matrix[³](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management#The_Eisenhower_Method) to figure out what to do with them.

The Eisenhower Matrix is a simple 2 by 2 grid that separates tasks into:

- **Important and urgent** tasks are the ones you should do first. Those could be tasks that bring high value, or that enable other tasks that are valuable
- **Important but not urgent** tasks are similar but can be planned for later. This helps you avoid starting with the wrong tasks and not meeting deadlines on others.
- **Not important** tasks may still be urgent. While they don’t contribute much to the project, they may have to be done. For example, to fulfill internal processes. If you can delegate them do so. Otherwise, I’d recommend working on them when your concentration is low. This allows you to spend your most productive time on the important tasks.
- If tasks are neither important nor urgent it probably means you can ignore them. Sometimes those tasks turn out to not be required anymore after some time and just disappear.

Over time you will internalize this method. At this point you don’t need to actually draw a matrix or even think about tasks anymore. You automatically *know* what to do with a task.

Until you can do this, I’d encourage you to start or end every week by drawing a matrix like this and sorting your tasks. You can use a whiteboard, a piece of paper or some digital tool. Personally, I prefer the haptic feel of real-world items, but this is up to you.

I would suggest doing this exercise at the end of the week. At this point you still have the context of all the tasks in mind. And it allows you to immediately start with the first task in the next week.

Combining the *value vs. effort* and the *Eisenhauer* matrix allows you to avoid tasks with a bad ROI[⁴](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_on_investment). Like automating things that seem important, but in the end take longer to automate than do manually. Instead you focus on tasks with an actual impact.

In summary, focusing on actual work and tasks with an impact will make you much more productive. You will get so much more done than your colleagues in most companies, it will feel like cheating. This has worked great for me, not only to achieve more, but also to enjoy my work more. It is more fun to put in the effort if you see actual results.

## 2. Always assume ignorance not malice

This statement, also referred to as Hanlon’s razor[⁵](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanlon%27s_razor), is a cautionary rule to avoid misinterpreting people’s actions. In his book, *How to win friends and influence people*[⁶](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Win_Friends_and_Influence_People), Dale Carnegie’s[⁷](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Carnegie) shares some similar ideas. For example, *to see things from the other person’s point of view* and *to be interested in the other person and their needs*.

Combining those three proverbs gives you a good way of approaching your colleagues.

Start out assuming that they think of their own needs, and this is what motivates their actions. Don’t assume they want to put you at a disadvantage. Try to understand where they are coming from. Once you realize they are not out to get you, you can show your genuine interest in their needs. This will help you create a connection with them, making it a lot easier to cooperate.

If you were to see their actions as an attack on you, you’ll close off any bond you may have with them. This will lead to a negative and aggressive relationship, making it hard to work together. This is especially problematic if you have continuous touch points at work. Without a good feeling towards each other, every interaction will be stressful. And you never achieve the best possible outcome for either one of you.

Additionally, I found that being constantly suspicious and fearing harm dampens your mood. You are defensive, aggressive, unhappy, and preoccupied. This makes it hard to perform well, be creative and can lead to stress or even burnout.

The worst part about this is that most of it only takes place in your head. Most folks spend the majority of their day thinking about themselves. This is the reason why they may do stuff that is not cool, they just forgot to think about you. But not out of malice, out of ignorance. Or because they were preoccupied with themselves and their worries.

If you think about this, it is really not worth losing any sleep over it. You win nothing but you can lose everything from assuming malice. So, make your life happy and assume ignorance, most of the time you will be correct.

## 3. Think in systems

Everything is part of a bigger system. The experience you have in an Apple store influences your perception of the brand and product quality. Just like any feature in an app impacts your perception of the entire app. It does not matter if you can influence the system or not, you need to be aware of the system to be most effective. This idea can be used in any environment and is not specific to design or development.

For example, by looking at the existing parts of a product, you can anticipate users’ expectations. By understanding the expectations and design language you can make a new feature fit in with the rest. You can also evaluate if a change on your end has or should have an impact outside of your feature.

The idea is not to avoid changing the system at all costs. But to evaluate if a change to the system makes sense, is feasible and the best use of the available resources. And if the answer is yes, you need to work with the folks responsible for changing the system.

However, you need to understand that a consistent system is always easier to use. Problems should be fixed, improvements should be made. But consistently across the entire system. Not just in the newly added parts.

Understanding the system you operate in also helps you understand why problems exist and what is needed to solve them. This can drastically increase your impact. Without looking at the system you may fail to actually solve the cause of a problem. Sometimes it is better to simplify a process than to make the form used for it prettier.

## 4. Make a list

This one is so simple, but so good. If I could only ever use one method for managing my life, it would surely be lists. I am a big fan of keeping the tools I use to a minimum. And lists work in nearly every tool and nearly every situation. Most of my lists are actually pen and paper. If you are not a project manager, this is probably all you need for organizing your life and work tasks.

The two main things I like to use lists for are:

- **remembering things**
- **making decisions**

## Remembering things

I use lists in two places, digitally within the project management my team uses and as pen and paper on my desk.

Generally, I use digital lists for larger tasks that have to be shared with my team. Often those are part of a kanban board[⁸](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanban_(development)) with issues/tickets. Each ticket has a list of tasks, requirements, etc. This makes them easy to scan and easy to edit. I know I won’t forget any of the items, if there is only one place to look for them.

On my desk, I have a couple notes with lists. It may look messy, but it is always in my field of view and can’t be hidden in a closed app. Those lists hold personal tasks I must complete but I also use them for work tasks.

I transfer the next steps for any project I work on onto a paper list. Sometimes I add subtasks that are not part of the digital version. This makes it easier to remember the steps. It also gives me a sense of accomplishment and progress when I can check something off. Motivation is key to being productive.

One of the most important aspects of those lists is that they give me peace of mind. I know, there are only two places where a task could be. I know I won’t forget it. This is very important for me as it frees up a lot of capacity in my brain. I can focus on the task at hand, rather than worrying about if I may have forgotten something. I know I checked both places, so there is nothing I am forgetting. Easy.

## Making decisions

Let’s assume you want to decide if you should move to a new framework for your web app.

The first step is to create a list with the pros and cons for each side of the decision.

For implementing a new framework, you should write down pros like what will you gain from a new framework? Will it make things easier, or allow you to use third party packages? And on the con side, what will you lose? And what will it take to implement the new framework? What risks are involved with changing the framework?

For keeping the old framework, consider pros like what you can do with the time that you would otherwise spend on implementing a new framework? Other benefits it may have. And for cons, you should consider things like no support from the creators. Plugins and packages dropping support so you can’t updated. Accumulating code dept that will make updating harder in the future. Risks involved with keeping the old framework. Maybe some things are much more time consuming in the old framework, etc.

Afterwards you can match up the lists. For example, compare the gains and the risks, the impact on your product or your team. Placing the choices next to each other like this makes it much easier to make a good decision.

If it is still hard, you can use a point system to weigh the pros and cons. I have done this before when I had a hard time with a decision. For example, an improved developer experience may only be 1 point, whereas continuing security updates may be 5 points.

I am sure there are many other things you can do with lists, but this is what I use them for. I find them so important because they are easy and quick to use. They don’t require any tool or software. They seem truly magical in their simplicity and effectiveness.

## 5. Small and steady beats anything

In his book *Atomic habits*[⁹](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits) James Clear describes how to use compound growth for self-development. By doing small steps every day or at least with a set cadence you get something like compound growth in the stock market. You will get better at whatever you try to do, which will make it easier to continue improving. I have tried this and found it to be one of the most valuable insights to achieving anything.

This method is not only helpful for habits, but also for any task. Often the problem is missing motivation or fear of an overwhelming task. But if you only commit to a small step every day, it becomes much less scary. Even if the task is daunting, the small step seems doable.

Instead of finishing a presentation, you can work on it for just 30 minutes a day. Instead of going for a 5k run, you start with a short run up and down your street. Once you start a task it is often easier to do more than you planned. And even if not, by showing up every day you will make progress. Anything is better than not starting.

At your job, this approach can work in your favor in another way. When working in teams you often must wait for other people. This means you can start a task, do some work, and ask for feedback or missing information. But instead of sitting idle, you focus on another small tasks. Your colleagues hopefully provide what is missing so the next day you can continue. This way you can overcome the fear of big tasks and work more efficiently at the same time.

Additionally, this will make you start your tasks early to be able to finish them in small steps. And this is a method I would recommend in any case. It is better to work on a task over a longer period, you’ll get a better idea of what and who is needed. And this leaves you time to think about your solutions and let ideas mature in the back of your head. Getting some distance from your own ideas can be very helpful at times and often makes for a better result.

## 6. Be helpful

Another thing I found is often overlooked is being helpful. This does not mean that you should do everything for everyone. Rather, there are two specific situations in which you should be helpful:

## It is your area of expertise

If there is a problem in an area you are the expert in or want to be recognized as the expert, you should help out. Let’s say a specific process or a software like Figma or Excel is your USP. If there is a complicated project or problem needing this expertise, get involved. For you it makes sense to help folks solve this problem, because it reinforces your expert image. For the company it also makes sense because it produces faster and better results.

However, being an expert shouldn’t make you afraid to teach colleagues. Often folks fear that they will lose their expert role if they help others get better. But this is rarely the case. In nearly all cases it means you have less work next time but are seen as even more of an expert. The reason is that teaching others requires an even better understanding of a topic than simply working on it.

Helping out like this often gets you known across your organization as an expert. This means other teams may ask for your expertise as well. While this adds more to your task list, it also makes you more valuable for the company. And it allows you to gain insight into what other teams are working on and make new connections.

## It is a small task

Helping out as a favor is a great way to build positive connections at work. Normally I would only really do this if it were a small amount of work. But depending on the person it is okay to spend a decent amount of time working on it. Either if they are nice and you can afford it, or if they are important for your career. By helping you are investing in a work relationship.

The one thing to keep an eye on is that you are not being used. This means, you should not do the work for someone who spends the time doing other work to seem extra productive. The person you help should be there with you, so you can help them solve their problem and not solve it for them.

You should also be careful if folks claim credit for your work. If you only helped a bit, it’s fine if you are not specifically mentioned. But if they talk about how they solved the specific problem without mentioning your help I would avoid them in the future.

Helping others can be good for you in many ways. First of all, it means you build positive connections, and this will often come back to you. Colleagues will talk about you being helpful or suggest you as an expert at times.

Additionally, it will create a network of folks who may be able to help you out when you need something. Maybe they have expert knowledge in another area or can help with a process or some information.

Lastly it will show that you are a team player. And you will get to know your colleagues better even if you don’t work on a project with them. This can even improve your day, as it’s more fun to work in a company where you know a lot of people.

## The Lie: Never be wrong

On of the biggest lies that is told to folks is to never be wrong. I feel this is hurting you in two ways.

If being wrong is so bad, people try to cover up their mistakes instead of owning them. If people know or find out that you messed up, trying to hide it will make you seem weak and untrustworthy. It will also create a situation in which mistakes are visible, but nobody knows why it happens. As a result, folks will not only be unhappy with the result now, but fear that this will happen again.

If instead you own the mistake, you may be able to provide a reason why this happend. And with this you can suggest a strategy to make sure this won’t happen again. This will most likely make you seem more competent in the end. Nobody is perfect so being able to deal with mistakes is a very important skill. It makes you seem like a leader in this situation. Even if it may be partly negative at the moment, people are likely to remember that you stood up and solved the issue. Of course, this only works if you actually do work on preventing this issue in the future.

Another problem with being afraid of making mistakes is that it will paralyze you. I have seen this in many companies where folks are so afraid of being wrong that they end up not doing anything. They have meetings, plannings and research sessions. All to make sure they are definitely not wrong and making no mistake. But in life there is no certainty. So they end up with more of the same meetings and never move on to actually doing something.

I am not saying you should jump in blindly. Definitely plan and do some research. But after an initial research phase you need to start working on the project. Even if you are not sure about everything. You can do more detailed research later, if needed. Definitely try to be successful with the first approach, failing is never fun. But don’t let this stop you from doing anything. If the stakes are high, you can start with tiny work packages. This is damage control in case you fail. This could be releasing something internally first. Or it could mean changing only a portion of a flow to a new pattern.

> *In short, it is better to fail at times but move forward, than to never fail, but fail to ever deliver anything.*

## Summary

In short, the most crucial tool you should remember is the simple list. It can help you make the right decisions and remember what to do.

I have two tips for good work relationships, and to be liked and valued by your colleagues and managers. First, assume that folks don’t want to harm you but instead are ignorant that their action disadvantages you. You will be in a better mood and avoid unnecessary problems. Second, help folks when it allows you to show your expertise. Or when a colleague will value your help and not claim it as their own achievement.

To learn and grow you shouldn’t let the fear of failure stop you from trying things. If you do fail, own it, but show how you will prevent it from happening again. When you learn new things or work on large tasks, do it in small steps. Small and steady makes it easier to motivate you and you stay flexible if something changes.

Lastly to make sure you move the needle with your work, you must understand that everything is part of a system. So, look at the impact your change has on the entire product or organization. Additionally, focus on actions rather than endless planning. And make sure whatever you do has the largest possible impact. Don’t spend all day on stuff that will go unnoticed.
